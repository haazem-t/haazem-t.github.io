Engineering

Keeping heavy industry standing up.

Mechanical engineering at the University of Michigan, two summers as a Department of Defense X-Force Fellow, and a term of renewable energy research in Berlin — then straight into the field. Today I work in asset integrity, which mostly means being on site, on the pipe, with instruments in my hands.

X-Force Fellow · 2022 & 2023

Two summers solving Defense Department problems.

The X-Force Fellowship puts small teams of students onto real national security problems for ten weeks, full-time, with a military sponsor. I was selected twice.

In 2022 the sponsor was the U.S. Air Force and the problem was Agile Combat Deployment: repair a damaged runway, or stand up a new one, in under eight hours. Six of us spent eight days at the Northern Agility 22-1 exercise in Michigan's Upper Peninsula, where a stretch of the M-28 highway became an active runway for seven hours and the Air Force performed the first full Integrated Combat Turn on a public highway in U.S. history. I interviewed civil engineers, firefighters and pilots at K.I. Sawyer, called equipment manufacturers including Hitachi and Bobcat about what their machines could actually do, and ran the numbers on how thick a surface has to be for a fighter to land on it and take off again. We delivered a proof of concept.

“My summer was straight out of a Hollywood movie.” — Michigan Center for Entrepreneurship, student spotlight

In 2023 I went back, this time with the U.S. Navy at Naval Surface Warfare Center, Crane Division, on a three-person team covering two problems: shielding microelectronics against radiation, and insulating conductors at cryogenic temperatures. I researched radiation damage in silicon wafers and ran molecular dynamics simulations of an aluminium lattice in LAMMPS. The deliverables aren't public.

  • Selected twice — U.S. Air Force (2022), U.S. Navy / NSWC Crane (2023)
  • Ten weeks each, full-time and paid
  • Field research, equipment scouting, surface load calculations, LAMMPS simulation

Current role · In the field

On site, on the pipe.

Piping in a refinery is never still. Pumps, compressors and flow turbulence shake it constantly, and small-bore connections — the little branch lines off the main run — are where that shaking does its damage first. I travel to the units, instrument those connections, and check them against API limits. When a line comes back over the limit, I design the fix: what to brace, where, and how, based on the pipe size, the configuration, and the data I just collected.

  • Accelerometer surveys on small-bore connections across live units
  • Screening against API acceptance criteria
  • Remediation design and written findings for the client
  • Around four sites a year — North Dakota, Kentucky, Minnesota, Michigan, California

NASA · 2026

Learning how NASA decides what to fund.

The NASA Proposal Writing and Evaluation Experience is a full-semester academy that takes you through the process from the inside: identify a NASA need, scope a response against a real solicitation, and write the proposal. I handled topic research, CAD modelling and the statistical analysis for my team's submission. We didn't place. Afterwards I was trained as a reviewer and scored four other teams' proposals — which taught me more about writing a good one than writing my own had.

  • Fifteen weeks, sponsored by NASA
  • Research, CAD modelling and statistical analysis on the team's proposal
  • Trained as a reviewer; scored four peer proposals

Research · TU Berlin · 2023

A term on renewables in Berlin.

A semester at Technische Universität Berlin working through how solar, wind and storage actually behave as a system rather than as separate technologies. The question I ended on was a practical one: what's the right way to store energy in different regions of the United States, given that geography decides so much of the answer.

  • Built the models in Python
  • Simulated complete renewable systems for three U.S. cities and compared them by geography
  • Worked within a German research group

Leadership

I started a club from an empty room.

There was no judo club at Michigan, so I built one — sourced the equipment, imported it, recruited the first members, and coached. Four years later it has seven team championships and around fifty members. I still coach it, unpaid, as a volunteer.

Founding the club

The mats came from China. I got them off the boat.

A judo club is a floor before it is anything else, and a domestic tatami order was far beyond what a new student group could fund. So I sourced the mats from a manufacturer in China, negotiated the order, arranged the freight, and handled the import paperwork, customs clearance and collection from the port myself — then laid the floor. It was the first project I ever ran end to end.

  • 64 m² of tatami — about 690 square feet — for roughly $5,000
  • Sourced and negotiated directly with an overseas manufacturer
  • Arranged international freight and import documentation
  • Cleared customs and collected the shipment from port
