2022 X-Force Fellowship · U.S. Air Force

Eight hours to build a runway.

The X-Force Fellowship places small teams of students with a Department of Defense sponsor for ten weeks, full-time and paid, on a problem the sponsor actually has. Mine came from the U.S. Air Force, and it was blunt: repair a damaged runway, or stand up a new one, in under eight hours.

Six of us worked it. Eight days of that summer were spent at the Northern Agility 22-1 exercise in Michigan's Upper Peninsula, watching a stretch of the M-28 highway become an active runway for nearly seven hours — the setting for the first full Integrated Combat Turn (refuelling and rearming a fighter) ever performed on a public highway in the United States.

My share of the work was the ground truth and the numbers. I interviewed Air Force civil engineers, firefighters and pilots at K.I. Sawyer about what actually slows a repair down. I called equipment manufacturers — Hitachi and Bobcat among them — to find out what their machines could really do in the field rather than in a brochure. And I ran the calculations on surface thickness: how much material a fighter needs beneath it to land and take off without tearing the surface apart.

We delivered a proof of concept at the end of the ten weeks.

2023 X-Force Fellowship · U.S. Navy, NSWC Crane

Radiation, cryogenics, and a lattice of aluminium.

I was selected a second time, now with the U.S. Navy at Naval Surface Warfare Center, Crane Division. Three of us, ten weeks, and two problems running in parallel: shielding microelectronics against radiation damage, and insulating conductors at cryogenic temperatures.

My work sat mostly on the first. I researched how radiation degrades electronics at the material level — what happens inside a silicon wafer when it's struck, and why that eventually shows up as a failed component. On the cryogenic side I ran molecular dynamics simulations of an aluminium lattice in LAMMPS to see how the structure behaves as temperature drops.

The deliverables aren't public.

2025 — present Field engineer · Asset integrity

On site, on the pipe.

Piping in a refinery is never still. Pumps, compressors and flow turbulence shake it constantly, and small-bore connections — the little branch lines coming off the main run — are where that shaking does its damage first. They're short, stiff, poorly supported, and they fail by fatigue long before the big line does.

I travel to the units, clamp accelerometers onto those connections while the plant is running, and record how much they're moving. The readings get screened against API acceptance criteria. Anything over the limit becomes my problem to solve: what to brace, where to brace it, and how — a judgement that depends on the pipe size, the configuration, and the data I just collected.

Roughly four sites a year so far. North Dakota, Kentucky, Minnesota, Michigan, California.

2023 Research · Technische Universität Berlin

Where you put the energy depends on where you are.

A semester at TU Berlin spent working through how solar, wind and storage behave as a single system rather than three separate technologies — how a panel actually produces, how a turbine actually produces, and what storage has to absorb to hold the two together.

The question I ended on was practical: what's the right way to store energy in different regions of the United States? Geography decides more of the answer than the technology does. I built the models in Python and simulated complete renewable systems for three U.S. cities, then compared them against each other on the basis of where they sit.

2026 NASA Proposal Writing and Evaluation Experience

Learning how NASA decides what to fund.

A full-semester, fifteen-week academy that runs you through NASA's proposal process from the inside: identify a genuine NASA need, scope a response against a real solicitation, and write the proposal as a team.

I handled the topic research, the CAD modelling and the statistical analysis for our submission. We didn't place. Afterwards I was trained as a reviewer and scored four other teams' proposals — which taught me considerably more about what makes a proposal work than writing our own had.
